Cold air outside, dry heat inside and long hot showers make winter hard on skin. Faces feel tight after cleansing, the neck gets flaky where a scarf rubs, and shins and hands can start to look ashy or rough. The good news is that a winter skincare routine for dry skin does not need to be complicated. It mostly means cleansing more gently, layering moisture more deliberately and protecting the skin barrier until spring. This guide walks through the face, the neck and the body, step by step, and explains why each change helps.

Quick answer

In winter, switch to a gentle, non-stripping cleanser, apply a humectant serum to slightly damp skin, then seal it in with a richer moisturizer or occlusive. Keep daily SPF, cut back on exfoliation, take shorter lukewarm showers, moisturize the body right after bathing, and run a humidifier where you sleep.

  • Gentle cleanser, once or twice a day, with lukewarm water
  • Humectant layer on damp skin, followed by a cream that seals it in
  • Broad-spectrum sunscreen every morning, including cloudy and snowy days
  • Fewer exfoliating steps and slower introduction of strong actives
  • Body moisturizer within a few minutes of drying off
  • Extra attention to the neck, hands, lips and feet

Key takeaways

  • Winter dryness is mostly about water leaving the skin faster, so the routine focuses on holding water in rather than adding more products.
  • The order that matters most is simple: gentle cleanse, humectant on damp skin, then a cream or balm on top.
  • Sunscreen stays in the morning routine all year, because UV exposure does not stop in winter.
  • The neck, hands and lips have thinner or more exposed skin and usually need their own step.
  • Persistent itching, cracking, bleeding or red patches are a reason to see a dermatologist rather than to keep adding products.

Why does skin get drier in winter?

The outer layer of skin works like a brick wall: skin cells are the bricks and natural lipids are the mortar that keeps water from escaping. In winter, several things work against that wall at once. Cold outdoor air holds very little moisture, and indoor heating dries the air further. When the air around you is dry, water evaporates from the skin surface more easily. Wind and friction from scarves, hats and coat collars add mechanical stress, especially around the jaw, neck and wrists.

Habits change too. Many people take longer, hotter showers when it is cold, and hot water dissolves more of the skin's own oils. Hand washing and sanitizer use often go up during cold and flu season. Put together, these factors explain why the routine that worked in July can leave your face tight and your shins flaky by December.

How should I change my skincare routine in winter?

You rarely need a brand-new routine. Most people get the biggest difference from five adjustments to what they already use. If you are starting from scratch, our complete guide to building a routine for your skin type covers the basic order of steps first, and our modern skincare routine guide explains how product categories and beauty tools fit together.

1. Swap to a gentler cleanser

Foaming or clarifying washes that felt fine in summer can leave dry skin tight in winter. A cream, milk or low-foam gel cleanser usually feels more comfortable. If your face feels squeaky or stretched after washing, that is a sign the cleanser is removing too much.

2. Add a humectant layer

Humectants such as glycerin and hyaluronic acid draw water into the outer skin layer. They work best on skin that is still slightly damp, for example right after cleansing.

3. Seal it in with a richer moisturizer

A humectant on its own can leave skin feeling tight again once the air dries it out. Following it with a cream that contains emollients and occlusive ingredients helps keep that water in. In winter, many people move from a gel or lotion to a thicker cream, at least at night.

4. Ease off exfoliation and strong actives

Scrubs, peel pads, strong acids and retinoids can all be harder to tolerate when the barrier is already stressed. You do not have to stop them completely, but using them less often, buffering them with moisturizer, or pausing them while skin is flaky or stinging is a common approach. If a dermatologist prescribed an active, ask them before changing how you use it.

5. Keep sunscreen in the morning

Daily broad-spectrum sunscreen stays in place. Winter sun is lower, but UV still reaches the skin, and snow can reflect it. There is more detail on this below.

Morning winter routine for dry facial skin

Keep the morning routine short so you actually do it. Here is a practical order for dry or dehydrated skin:

  1. Rinse with lukewarm water, or use a small amount of gentle cleanser if your skin feels oily or you used a heavy night cream. Pat dry with a clean towel, leaving the skin slightly damp.
  2. Apply a humectant serum, such as a hyaluronic acid serum like The Ordinary Hyaluronic Acid 2% + B5, to the face and front of the neck.
  3. Follow with a moisturizer while the serum is still slightly tacky. A face cream such as La Roche-Posay Toleriane Double Repair Face Moisturizer is one widely available option; check the ingredient list against your own sensitivities.
  4. Finish with a broad-spectrum sunscreen on the face, neck, ears and the backs of the hands. Give it a few minutes to set before makeup.
  5. Apply a lip balm, and reapply it through the day, especially before going outside.

Evening winter routine for dry facial skin

The evening routine is where you can be more generous with moisture, because you are not putting makeup or sunscreen on top.

  1. Remove sunscreen and makeup with a cleansing balm, oil or micellar water, then follow with a gentle cleanser if you need a second step.
  2. Pat the skin almost dry and apply your humectant serum.
  3. If you use a treatment product, such as a retinoid or an exfoliating acid, apply it on the nights you have planned for it. On dry or irritated nights, skip it.
  4. Apply a richer cream to the face and neck. On very dry areas, such as the sides of the nose or around the mouth, you can add a thin layer of a balm or ointment on top.
  5. Use a thicker lip product overnight and a hand cream before bed.

Humectants, emollients and occlusives: what does each one do?

Ingredient labels can be confusing, but most moisturizing ingredients fall into three groups. A good winter routine uses all three, either in separate products or in one well-formulated cream.

Winter neck care: don't stop at the jawline

The neck gets less attention than the face, yet it is exposed to the same cold air and often to more friction from scarves and collars. The skin on the front of the neck is relatively thin, and it can show dryness, crepiness or irritation from wool quickly.

  • Extend every face step down. Whatever cleanser, serum, moisturizer and sunscreen you use on your face, carry it down to the collarbone and around the sides of the neck.
  • Use upward, gentle strokes. Apply products with light pressure rather than tugging the skin.
  • Choose soft layers against the skin. A cotton or silk layer under a wool scarf can reduce rubbing.
  • Consider a dedicated neck product if you want one. Some people prefer a separate neck cream, such as Dr. Speron's Natural Skin Care Age Reversal Neck Cream. Read its ingredient list and directions on the product page before use, patch test first, and introduce it slowly if your neck is already dry or irritated, since products aimed at firmness can contain active ingredients.

Body care for dry winter skin: shower, hands, lips and feet

Body skin often gets driest of all in winter, especially on the shins, forearms, elbows and knees. Small changes to the shower routine make the biggest difference here.

Shower and bath habits

  • Keep showers and baths short and use lukewarm rather than hot water.
  • Use a mild, fragrance-free body wash, and only where you need it. Plain water is often enough on the shins and arms.
  • Pat the skin with a towel instead of rubbing it dry.
  • Apply body moisturizer within a few minutes, while skin is still a little damp. A rich body cream such as the Uzari Butterly Radiance Repair Cream is one option; confirm its ingredients and size on the product page.
  • Go easy on loofahs and body scrubs while skin is flaky. Gentle exfoliation once a week at most is plenty for most people in winter.

Hands

Hands are washed more than any other area and are often uncovered outdoors. Keep a hand cream next to every sink you use regularly and apply it after each wash. Wear gloves outside, and waterproof gloves for dish washing or cleaning. At night, a thicker cream under cotton gloves can help very dry hands.

Lips

Lips have no oil glands of their own, so they dry quickly in cold, windy weather. Use a plain balm through the day and a thicker layer at night. Try not to lick your lips, because saliva evaporates and leaves them drier. In daylight, a lip product with SPF adds sun protection.

Feet, heels, elbows and knees

These areas have thicker skin that can crack when dry. A rich cream or ointment applied at night, followed by cotton socks, is a common routine for heels. For rough elbows and knees, regular moisturizer works better than aggressive scrubbing. Deep, painful cracks in the heels, or any cracks that look infected, should be checked by a doctor or podiatrist, particularly if you have diabetes or poor circulation.

Your home environment: humidifiers, heat and clothing

What happens around your skin matters as much as what you put on it. A few changes at home support every product step above.

  • Use a humidifier. Running a humidifier in the bedroom adds moisture to the air while you sleep. Follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions closely and change the water as directed, because a dirty humidifier can spread mold or bacteria into the air.
  • Don't sit right next to the heat. Space heaters, fireplaces and car vents blowing on your face dry skin quickly.
  • Choose fabrics with care. Soft cotton and smooth layers next to the skin are usually more comfortable than rough wool directly on dry patches.

Do you still need sunscreen in winter?

Yes. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends daily sunscreen year-round, and winter is no exception. UV rays reach the skin on cloudy days, snow can reflect sunlight back up at your face, and UV exposure increases at higher altitude, which matters if you ski or hike in the mountains.

In winter, look for a broad-spectrum sunscreen with SPF 30 or higher, as dermatology guidance commonly advises, and apply it as the last step of your morning routine. Many people with dry skin find cream textures more comfortable than gels or sprays in cold weather. Remember the ears, the back of the neck and the backs of the hands, which are easy to miss under hats and gloves.

Where do sculpting tools and facial devices fit in winter?

If you already use a sculpting wand, roller or facial toning device, winter does not mean you have to stop. It does mean paying more attention to glide and to how your skin feels on the day.

  • Always use a slip product, such as a facial oil, serum or moisturizer, so the tool moves without dragging on dry skin.
  • Skip tool sessions on cracked, flaking, windburned or irritated areas until they settle.
  • Use lighter pressure than usual if your skin feels tight or sensitive.
  • Follow the session length and cleaning advice in your tool's instructions.

Common winter skincare mistakes

  • Long, hot showers: they feel good in the cold but strip natural oils; keep water lukewarm and showers short.
  • Applying moisturizer to bone-dry skin: moisturizer works better on slightly damp skin; apply it soon after washing.
  • Using a humectant alone in very dry air: without a cream on top, the water it attracts can evaporate again; follow with a sealing layer.
  • Over-exfoliating flaky skin: flakes are tempting to scrub, but scrubbing often makes dryness worse; moisturize first and exfoliate gently and rarely.
  • Dropping sunscreen until summer: UV exposure continues in winter; keep SPF in the morning routine.
  • Forgetting the neck and hands: these areas are exposed and often skipped; extend your routine to them.

When should you see a dermatologist?

Most winter dryness improves within a couple of weeks of gentler habits and better moisturizing. Some signs suggest something more than ordinary dry skin, and it is worth seeing a board-certified dermatologist or your doctor if you notice:

  • Red, itchy, scaly or rough patches that keep coming back, which can look like eczema
  • Itching that keeps you awake or does not ease with moisturizer
  • Skin that cracks, bleeds, oozes or looks infected
  • Dryness over large areas of the body, or dryness that started suddenly
  • Thick, silvery or well-defined plaques
  • No improvement after two to three weeks of consistent care

A dermatologist can work out whether you are dealing with simple dry skin or a condition such as eczema, psoriasis or contact dermatitis, and can recommend or prescribe treatment that a cosmetic routine cannot provide.

Bottom line

A good winter skincare routine for dry skin is less about buying more and more about doing a few things consistently. Cleanse gently with lukewarm water, put a humectant on damp skin, seal it in with a richer cream, and keep broad-spectrum sunscreen in the morning. Carry every step down to your neck, moisturize your body within minutes of showering, and give your hands, lips and feet their own small habits. Add a humidifier at home and ease off exfoliation until the weather warms up. If dryness turns into persistent itching, cracking or red patches, see a dermatologist rather than adding yet another product.

Frequently asked questions

How should I change my skincare routine in winter?

Use a gentle cleanser, apply a humectant serum to damp skin, then seal it in with a richer moisturizer. Keep daily sunscreen, exfoliate less, take shorter lukewarm showers and moisturize your body right after bathing.

What is the best order to apply skincare for dry skin in winter?

Cleanse, apply a humectant serum to slightly damp skin, then moisturizer. Finish with sunscreen in the morning. At night, treatment products go after the serum, and a thin balm can go on very dry patches last.

Should I use hyaluronic acid in winter?

It can help because it draws water into the skin, but it works best on damp skin under a moisturizer. On its own in very dry air, it can leave skin feeling tight.

Do I need sunscreen in winter if it is cloudy?

Yes. UV still reaches the skin on cloudy days and snow can reflect sunlight. Dermatology guidance recommends broad-spectrum SPF 30 or higher every day, all year.

How often should I exfoliate dry skin in winter?

Less often than in summer. Many people cut back to gentle exfoliation once a week, or pause it while skin is flaky or stinging. Ask your dermatologist before changing a prescribed product.

How can I stop my neck and hands from getting dry in winter?

Carry every face step, including sunscreen, down to the collarbone. Apply hand cream after every wash, wear gloves outdoors and use a thicker cream at night.

Does a humidifier help dry skin?

A humidifier adds moisture to dry, heated indoor air, which can make skin more comfortable, especially overnight. Clean it as the manufacturer directs.

When should I see a dermatologist for dry winter skin?

See one for recurring red, itchy or scaly patches, itching that disrupts sleep, skin that cracks or bleeds, or no improvement after two to three weeks of gentle care.
